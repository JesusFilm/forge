# The Caption Testing Program

The point is to end six months knowing things you didn't know at the start — not to have written six months of captions.

Contents:

1. The one-variable rule
2. Variables to test
3. Monthly cadence
4. Labeling rules
5. Reading performance data

---

## 1. The one-variable rule

Whenever possible, change one thing between the two captions you're comparing and hold everything else steady.

**Good test:** short caption vs. long caption, same hook concept, same audience, same CTA. If one wins, you learned something about length.

**Bad test:** short Gen-Z seeker-focused question caption vs. long believer-focused Scripture-heavy statement caption. If one wins, you learned nothing — four variables moved at once.

This is the whole discipline. It's also the easiest thing to quietly abandon when a caption idea is exciting. Write the exciting caption as a creative variation and keep the test clean.

## 2. Variables to test

### Caption length

- **Short** — ~30–60 words. Let the video carry it.
- **Medium** — ~60–120 words. Default range.
- **Long** — ~120–200 words. Room for reflection, context, searchable language.

_Question:_ does caption depth improve saves and engagement, or does concise copy win on watch behavior?

### Hook style

Question · bold statement · contradiction · relatable experience · Bible-story curiosity · direct challenge · felt need · Scripture-centered observation.

> Question: "What do you do when fear arrives before the answer?"
> Statement: "Fear has a way of filling the silence while we wait for God."

_Question:_ does an open question outperform a declarative observation?

### Audience faith position

**Seeker-friendly** — assume little Bible knowledge, possible non-Christian. No insider terminology. Enough context to make the idea land. Invite curiosity rather than assume agreement.

**Believer-focused** — assume familiarity with Jesus, Scripture, prayer, discipleship. Can press directly on obedience, trust, surrender, spiritual formation.

_Question:_ does accessible language expand reach without thinning engagement?

### Generational voice

**Younger** — faster, tighter, tension early, minimal explanation, emotionally direct, skimmable. The difference is rhythm and framing, never slang. Manufactured slang reads as condescension and is worse than writing plainly.

**Broader / older** — more context, more reflective pacing, clearer Scripture, less reliance on implied meaning.

_Question:_ does conversational speed resonate differently from reflective devotional pacing?

### Men vs. women

Only test when the devotional genuinely supports different framings. Never lean on clichés (men = strength/work/sports, women = emotions/relationships) — those are lazy and they insult the audience.

Legitimate framing differences to test: responsibility, fear, control, relationships, identity, approval, waiting, purpose, anxiety, trust.

If gendered framing would feel artificial for this passage, skip the test. A forced test produces a bad caption _and_ bad data.

### Felt need vs. Bible-first

**Felt-need-first** — start in the viewer's experience, then connect to Scripture.

> "What do you do with the fear that shows up before the answer does?"

**Bible-first** — start in the biblical moment, then connect to the viewer.

> "The disciples had Jesus in the boat — and were still terrified of the storm."

_Question:_ does everyday experience pull more viewers in than Scripture does?

### Direct challenge vs. gentle invitation

**Challenge:** "It's easy to say you trust God until trusting Him costs you control."
**Invitation:** "If you've been waiting on God, this story may be worth sitting with."

Watch for a split result: challenge often lifts retention and comments while invitation lifts shares and saves. Both are real wins; which matters depends on the goal that month.

### YouTube title style

Question · reversal · named-story · direct challenge · felt need. See `references/youtube.md` for what each looks like.

_Question:_ does a searchable named-story title pull more clicks than a tension-driven one — and does it pull the _right_ viewers, or just more of them?

Judge this on click-through rate and average view duration together. A title that lifts clicks while dropping watch time promised something the video doesn't deliver.

### Cover text vs. title relationship

**Echo** — cover text restates the title's core idea in fewer words.
**Complement** — cover text names a different piece of the tension than the title does.

_Question:_ does a complementary pair outperform an echoing one?

Only run this when you can hold the title steady across two thumbnails, which usually means the same video posted to two surfaces, or a title kept while the thumbnail is swapped. Otherwise it's not a clean test.

**A caution specific to YouTube:** title and thumbnail move click-through far more than caption copy ever moves Instagram performance. That makes results here easier to read — and also means a YouTube result tells you nothing about what works in an Instagram caption. Keep the two sets of findings separate in the log.

### Explicit Christian language

**High** — Jesus, Bible, Scripture, God, faith, prayer appear early.
**Lower** — human tension first, spiritual context arrives a beat later.

This tests _when_ the spiritual frame appears, not whether it appears. Never disguise that the content is Christian.

## 3. Monthly cadence

Approximate. Adapt when data points somewhere sooner.

| Month | Focus                                                                                                                          |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| 1     | **Baseline.** Challenge vs. curiosity; question vs. statement hooks. Hold everything else steady.                              |
| 2     | **Length.** Short vs. medium vs. long, similar audience and message.                                                           |
| 3     | **Faith audience.** Seeker-friendly vs. believer-focused vs. balanced.                                                         |
| 4     | **Generational framing.** Younger rhythm vs. broad vs. reflective adult.                                                       |
| 5     | **Emotional framing.** Men/women where appropriate; felt need vs. Bible-first; challenge vs. invitation.                       |
| 6     | **Combine winners.** Best hook + best length. Best audience framing + best CTA. Best SEO approach + strongest retention style. |

Don't test everything every week. One live experiment at a time is plenty.

## 4. Labeling rules

Two different things, and conflating them ruins the log:

- **Creative variation** — a different way of saying the same thing. Normal, good, not an experiment.
- **Structured test** — one variable deliberately changed to learn something.

Only the second gets a test label. If a caption isn't part of an experiment, write `Test: Baseline / no intentional experiment` and move on.

Testing labels never appear inside the caption. The caption must be clean to paste.

## 5. Reading performance data

When the user shares numbers, group them by what they actually tell you:

**YouTube-specific** — impressions, click-through rate, average view duration, average percentage viewed. Click-through rate judges the title and thumbnail pair. Average view duration judges whether they told the truth.

**Watch performance** — views, reach, 3-second views, average watch time, completion rate, replays. This judges hooks and retention strategy. Weight it most.

**Shares + saves** — judges whether the devotional felt meaningful or useful enough to keep or pass along. This is the closest proxy for spiritual resonance.

**Profile visits + follows** — judges audience fit and interest in the series as a whole.

Likes and comments are the noisiest. Comments are worth reading qualitatively even when the count is small.

Guard against three mistakes:

1. **Declaring winners on thin data.** A 4% difference on two Reels is noise. Look for the same pattern across several posts.
2. **Attributing caption results to the caption.** Video quality, posting time, audio choice, thumbnail, and algorithmic luck all move these numbers more than caption copy does. A caption test is a weak signal by nature — treat repeated patterns as suggestive, not proven.
3. **Ignoring inconclusive results.** "No detectable difference" is a real finding and belongs in the log. It means stop spending attention there.

---

## 6. Choosing the next test

Ask, in order:

1. Does the current month's focus still have an untested variant? Finish it.
2. Did the last result raise a sharper question than the one you started with? Follow it — the calendar is a default, not a rule.
3. Is there a variable with no data at all? Prefer it over re-testing something already settled.
4. Would this test produce a bad caption? If so, skip it. Publishing weak content to satisfy a testing schedule is a bad trade.

State the next test in one line so it's easy to act on next session.
